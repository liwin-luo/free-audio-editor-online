"432 Hz music" is a recording whose A above middle C sits at 432 Hz. The
international standard is 440 Hz, fixed by ISO 16 in 1975 and agreed at a
London conference in 1939. Converting a song from 440 to 432 moves every
frequency in the file down by one factor, 432 over 440, which is 31.8 cents,
about a third of a semitone. The length does not change. The
[432 Hz converter](/432-hz-converter) does exactly that shift.

This page is the audit around it: what 432 Hz actually is, where 440 came
from, what the claims for 432 are, what the only controlled study measured,
and what a conversion can and cannot do to a file you already have.

## What is 432 Hz?

432 Hz is a tuning reference, not a genre and not a frequency your music
"contains". In a recording tuned to 432 Hz, the A above middle C — the note
orchestras tune to — vibrates 432 times per second. On almost all modern
recordings that same A vibrates 440 times, the value ISO 16 fixed as the
standard in 1975. Everything else follows from that one difference: a song
"converted to 432 Hz" is the same song with every frequency multiplied by
432/440, which sits about a third of a semitone lower. When people say
"432 Hz music" they mean a recording tuned that way — in practice almost
always an ordinary 440 recording shifted down after the fact, which is what
the [432 Hz converter](/432-hz-converter) does.

## Where 440 came from, and where 432 was

440 Hz is not ancient. Scheibler proposed it in 1834 and a Stuttgart scientific
society approved it, but orchestras kept tuning where their instruments and
halls put them: French standard 435, British 439, baroque pitch 415, "classical"
estimates between 427 and 430. Delegates from five European countries agreed on
440 at the BBC's Broadcasting House in May 1939, partly because 439, the British
tuners' frequency since 1899, is a prime number, and 440 divides and synthesizes
more easily. ISO took it up as Recommendation R 16 in 1955 and formalized it as
ISO 16 in 1975.

So the honest history is the opposite of the usual story. Tuning was never
universally 432 anywhere, and 440 won for a practical reason: the number is
easy to generate.

## The claims, and the one study that tested them

Search "432 Hz" and the promises arrive fast: calmer, healthier, more in tune
with the body, the "ancient" or "Verdi" frequency. Before 2019, a search for
controlled evidence returned nothing, which the authors of the first controlled
experiment state in their own background section.

That experiment is a double-blind cross-over pilot: 33 volunteers, two
20-minute sessions on different days, the same movie soundtracks, one day at
440, one at 432. The measurements: heart rate dropped 4.79 beats per minute
more after the 432 session, with p = 0.05, which is the border of conventionally
significant. Blood pressure moved a little and not significantly. Breathing
rate moved slightly, p = 0.06. Subjects reported slightly more focus. A
corrigendum followed in 2020.

That is the entire controlled literature: one pilot, borderline numbers, no
replication. It does not show harm, and it does not come close to supporting
"healing". If a new study appeared tomorrow with the same design and no
difference, nobody could call it surprising.

## What a conversion does to the file

This part is arithmetic, not opinion. The shifter multiplies every frequency
by 432/440, which is 0.9818. A 440 Hz A lands on 432 Hz. Middle C moves from
261.6 to 256.9 Hz. The whole spectrum moves together, instruments included,
down about a third of a semitone, and the duration is untouched, because this
is a pitch shift, not a speed change.

Whether a third of a semitone is audible depends on the material and on having
both versions side by side. My own A/B on a full mix says: in isolation I could
not name the tuning; against the original, the 432 copy sounds very slightly
darker. That is a preference, not a measurement, and you can run the same test
on your file in under a minute.

## What a conversion does not do

It does not retune individual notes. A chord is not pulled onto 432 note by
note; the recording moves as one block. It does not clean up a bad recording,
restore lost detail, or undo a lossy encode; the WAV export is the shifted
samples, and the MP3 export encodes them once, up to 320 kbps. And no converter
makes a file "heal": the one controlled study above is the evidence, and it
claims much less than the videos do.

## Reasons that survive the audit

Convert when a musical reason is real: a catalog you are keeping at 432, a
loop that has to match tracks already shifted, or you simply prefer how the
darker pitch sits. Preference needs no study. Convert when a video told you
it will calm you, and you will be acting on a pilot of 33 people, not a
finding.

## The verdict

440 is a 20th-century agreement, not a law of physics, and nothing stops you
from tuning down. The measurable cost is a third of a semitone and one MP3
re-encode. The measurable benefit is a heart-rate delta at p = 0.05 in a
33-person pilot. Convert because you like the sound. That reason is enough,
and for now it is the only one with clean hands.
